If the academic iron rice bowl breaks, do young scholars get a seat?

I support holding faculty to their responsibilities. It is hard to defend intense scrutiny of junior staff while people who control resources need not explain their contribution. But stricter review alone cannot establish a reform's value. We also need to follow the resources and ask whether younger scholars gain room to work independently.

Separate the notice from the prediction

Tongji University's School of Economics and Management notice for the 2025 annual review specifies differentiated assessment and links to performance-related decisions. It covers the school's December faculty and staff, excluding postdoctoral researchers, with stated exceptions. It sets out consequences for review outcomes. This establishes meaningful obligations, not nationwide abolition of tenure or impending mass departures of older professors.

Annual assessment, appointment-term review, and evaluation before obtaining tenure are different processes. Claims about reform need the applicable staff category, existing terms, and procedure. This essay forecasts no dismissal percentage and does not treat online commentary as an adopted university policy.

Accountability requires evidence, not age labels

Persistent failure to fulfill teaching, research, or service duties while retaining resources warrants scrutiny. The test should concern verifiable responsibilities and contributions. An inability to code does not establish uselessness; a sparse publication record alone does not establish neglect. Disciplines, roles, and research cycles differ.

Teaching foundational courses, maintaining shared facilities, and accumulating engineering knowledge take time. Rewarding only near-term paper counts can discourage those activities. Youth does not guarantee excellence, and seniority does not establish idleness. Moral judgments based on age are no better than exemptions based on rank.

Stable appointments should support long research projects and uncomfortable questions while carrying defined duties. Academic protection and responsibility can coexist.

An alliance between leaders and juniors is a hypothesis

University leaders seeking stronger research and junior scholars seeking opportunities can have common interests. That does not establish a coordinated alliance across institutions or prove that a numerically dominant middle group contributes little.

Senior researchers, administrators, and team members may clash or depend on long collaboration. Mid-career faculty perform varied teaching, management, and research work. Rules and resource movements offer better evidence of reform coalitions than dividing everyone into three boxes.

Downward transfer of pressure needs safeguards

Where a few people control laboratories, student places, and recommendations, tighter review may encourage them to assign more work to junior colleagues. Authorship disputes or appropriation of contributions require independent handling. These are risks for institutional design, not allegations against a named university or an age group.

A junior researcher with little power to refuse an unreasonable task does not experience shared pressure on equal terms. Equal numbers on an assessment form do not imply equal bargaining power.

A vacancy does not guarantee an independent opportunity

A university might redirect a vacancy's budget toward junior startup funding, established teams, different roles, or spending cuts. Without an explicit allocation, none of these outcomes can be promised. Evaluation should follow redistributed resources rather than stop at the number of unsuccessful reviews.

I would look for funds junior staff can control, fair access to laboratory space and student supervision, renewal terms explained at entry, and transition arrangements when rules change. These determine whether someone gains a career opportunity or a tighter task list.

Make reform's promises testable

Role-based assessment should reflect actual work. Teaching needs evidence about classroom quality and student development. Research needs peer judgment and attention to its timescale. Treatment of care responsibilities and illness should be explicit and predictable. Requirements for improvement and subsequent decisions should leave a reviewable record.

Evaluators should recuse themselves from conflicts of interest, and appeals should extend beyond the original decision-maker. Team achievements need an account of individual contributions. Universities should examine teaching quality, junior retention, and independent development after reform, not only aggregate paper counts.

Prospective hires can ask for written duties, available resources, renewal procedures, and appeal routes. Transferable research and engineering skills help, but advice to improve oneself cannot excuse an institution from treating staff fairly.

I support removing protection for persistent nonperformance while protecting people who do their jobs, including senior colleagues. Junior opportunity should bring independence in resources, authorship, and judgment. A reform that only makes everyone busier has yet to deliver its most worthwhile promise.
