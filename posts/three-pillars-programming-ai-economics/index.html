Are the three pillars of programming collapsing? AI, oversupply, and the economics of “good-enough” code

I am less worried about AI making programming skills useless than about it making familiar work cheaper. A company may still need domain knowledge, debugging, and readable code while paying less for the time spent on them. That distinction matters to anyone whose income depends on those skills.

Experience helps a programmer notice an unwritten business rule, find the cause of a failure, or keep a codebase manageable. Employers have paid for those abilities partly because they are costly to acquire. As models take on portions of the work, we need to examine which costs they remove and which they leave with the engineer.

A model might retrieve a rule that once took a newcomer days to find. That does not mean it can decide whether the rule applies to a disputed transaction. Treating both tasks as “domain expertise” hides the part of the job that is becoming easier to replace.

The first pillar: domain knowledge becomes context engineering

Experience in payment idempotency, ledger reconciliation, healthcare compliance, or supply-chain exceptions once created a substantial information gap. A newcomer did not know what to search for or when a general rule in the documentation would fail.

With access to the relevant tools and documents, a model can retrieve standards, read code history, and use project instructions such as AGENTS.md. Teams can also supply incident reviews and business checklists. Writing this knowledge down makes it available to colleagues and agents instead of leaving each newcomer to discover it again.

Some constraints remain unwritten: why a partner follows a special rule, whether a metric is trustworthy this week, or who must approve a risky change. Models cannot reliably recover missing context from a generic specification. An experienced engineer still has to identify those gaps, consult the people involved, and judge whether the proposed solution fits.

The second pillar: debugging becomes instrumented search

An experienced engineer can use logs, latency curves, and recent releases to choose where to investigate first. That saves time because reading every available record is rarely practical for a person. Agents make a wider search feasible, which changes the advantage that comes from knowing where to look.

Sentry, Datadog, distributed tracing, and MCP tools have changed the cost of search. An agent can read stacks, compare changes, run tests, query metrics, and check hypotheses along the call graph. Work that once demanded patience and memory can now run in batches. The personal advantage of “searching logs and trying a few fixes” will narrow.

An agent cannot inspect a state the system never logged. It may also misread a metric or miss a cross-team agreement that explains unusual behaviour. Engineers still need to design useful instrumentation, check causal explanations, and decide whether a rollback will help users or cause further harm.

The third pillar: code taste faces commercial pricing

The claim that “code is for models now, so humans no longer need to read it” is attractive and inaccurate. People still read code during incidents, audits, security reviews, and handoffs. Models also benefit from clean boundaries, stable interfaces, and predictable names. Disorganized code consumes more context, spreads changes across more files, and raises verification costs.

Buyers' willingness to pay is changing. A disposable script, short-lived prototype, or low-risk internal tool often needs to run and remain easy to replace. A team will not buy textbook architecture for every page. Long-lived infrastructure, financial ledgers, and clinical systems have different economics. Design costs avoided today can return during the next migration, audit, or outage.

I would judge design effort against the expected life and risk of the software. A tool that can be discarded tomorrow deserves a different review from a service holding years of customer state. Clear code remains useful in both cases, but the justification for extra architecture should be the maintenance or failure cost it avoids.

The evidence does not give one universal answer

Coding agents are taking on more execution. Anthropic analyzed 500,000 coding interactions from April 2025 and classified 79% of Claude Code conversations as “automation.” That label describes an interaction pattern that can still include error reports, human review, and repeated correction. It cannot be converted into a percentage of jobs that are ready for replacement. The original report documents these limits.

Productivity studies are less tidy. In METR's randomized study, 16 experienced open-source developers completed 246 tasks in repositories they knew well. Tasks using early-2025 AI tools took 19% longer on average. METR later marked that result as outdated. Its 2026 follow-up found signals of a possible speedup, but participant-selection effects prevented a reliable estimate of the current magnitude. Neither number justifies a verdict on the whole industry.

DORA 2025, an observational study of nearly 5,000 technology professionals, associated AI adoption with higher delivery throughput and product performance, while also associating it with lower delivery stability. Correlation does not prove causation. The result still shows why writing faster and delivering better belong to different scorecards. Tests, version control, and feedback loops determine whether a local speed gain becomes a system gain.

Jevons paradox will not protect every job

One optimistic argument invokes Jevons paradox: cheaper software production will lead society to consume more software, so demand for programmers will rise. The first part is plausible. School departments, small shops, and individuals will commission tools that were once too expensive to build.

The employment effect depends on how much new demand lower prices create and how companies divide the work. A company might use the saved time to build another product, or use it to reduce staffing. In some standardized tasks, one employee may do work that once needed several people. “One person replaces ten” is a scenario to test, not a general employment statistic.

Copywriting and basic UI work offer a useful comparison. Generative tools lower the price of standardized deliverables and give more people access to competent output. Demand may grow while supply grows faster, reducing the bargaining power of ordinary work. Programming tasks that are easy to specify, check, and copy may follow the same path.

An abundance of competent generalists changes who captures profit

AI helps more people cross the entry threshold. Product managers can build prototypes, researchers can write data pipelines, and independent developers can maintain products that once needed a small team. Society may receive more software without each worker earning more. When competent output becomes abundant, customers treat it as the default rather than paying a scarcity premium.

Companies that control models, cloud platforms, or distribution can charge for access. Engineers with customer relationships or responsibility for difficult, high-risk systems may retain more bargaining power than those selling standard code by the hour. This is a reason to watch who captures the savings; it does not support a precise claim that only “one percent” will benefit.

The new sources of scarcity

For a programmer deciding what to learn, I would pay attention to work the tools do not resolve on their own. That includes checking whether a requested feature solves the customer's problem, finding missing business constraints, and planning how to recover if a change fails. These tasks require contact with the people who use and operate the software.

A team also needs a record of why it defined a requirement that way, what people and models changed, and which risks the tests address. Someone must be able to stop an unsafe release. AI can help maintain those records, but generating more code without reviewing it only adds to the backlog of unverified work.

I do not expect these three skills to lose value at the same rate. Retrieving documented rules is easier to automate than resolving an undocumented exception. Searching logs is easier than deciding which explanation warrants a production change. The practical response is to use the tools for the cheaper work while learning to make and check the judgments that remain.
