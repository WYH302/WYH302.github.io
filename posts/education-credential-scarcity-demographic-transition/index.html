Education and society · Published September 7, 2026 at 17:56 (UTC+8)

Universities are not disappearing. The era of the scarce ordinary degree is ending.

Fewer postgraduate exam registrations, shrinking kindergartens, expanding doctoral cohorts, and contested academic careers belong to a longer reassessment of education. Universities will not disappear because several indicators turn downward. But the scarcity once attached to an ordinary degree is no longer a safe premise for planning a career.

I keep encountering the same chain of reasoning: fewer people register for the master's entrance exam; even prestigious universities advertise transfer places; falling births will eventually empty universities, just as they have reduced kindergarten enrolment. Add competitive faculty appointments, and the story ends with academics losing their jobs en masse.

Parts of that account rest on facts. The trouble lies in connecting registrations, enrolment, birth cohorts, and faculty recruitment as though they measured the same thing on the same timetable. I am more concerned about widening differences between institutions and jobs, and about people still making commitments on the assumption that expansion will continue.

1. The postgraduate exam has cooled; the investment deserves another look

Registrations for China's national master's entrance examination have declined for three consecutive years:

The decline from 4.74 million to 3.43 million amounts to 1.31 million, or about 27.6%. Meanwhile, master's admissions increased from 1.1035 million in the 2022 education bulletin to 1.2368 million in the 2025 bulletin.

Dividing admissions by registrations would not produce a valid national acceptance rate. Registration differs from attendance, admissions include routes such as recommendation without the entrance examination, and examination and intake years need alignment. The figures establish different directions in registration and training volumes. They do not show that every subject has become easier to enter or that nobody wants postgraduate education.

I am more interested in how applicants make decisions. Tuition, the length of the course, the city, supervision, and graduate destinations deserve separate scrutiny. What training will three years provide? Which work opportunities would you give up? Can you afford the commitment? Falling registrations alone cannot reveal everyone's motives, but they challenge the assumption that demand for another degree can only increase.

2. Transfer vacancies do not mean open admission

The University of Science and Technology of China's 2026 transfer notice identifies a small number of vacancies in particular programmes. Its School of Nuclear Science and Technology notice specifies eligibility, subject background, academic results, and further assessment. These notices establish vacancies in selected programmes, not a university-wide inability to recruit, and certainly not admission on demand.

A university's reputation cannot answer every question about its programmes. Subject, full-time or part-time study, fees, location, and employment options affect applicants' choices. Intense competition in one programme can coexist with transfer vacancies in another at the same institution.

I still consider the institutional platform important. I would not let its name settle the decision before examining the training and opportunities on offer. Comparing concrete programmes is more useful than arguing whether the prestige label of a former Project 985 university has retained its magic.

3. Kindergarten contraction is already visible, but it is uneven

The Ministry of Education's 2022 and 2025 statistical bulletins show substantial changes in preschool education.

Using those published totals, the net declines were about 19.8%, 30.3%, and 19.5%. A net reduction in institutions is not a list of bankruptcies. A change in teacher numbers is not a redundancy count: mergers, transfers, retirement, new entrants, and statistical changes require separate examination.

Nor will every classroom shrink from thirty children to twenty-eight at the same time. Migration and family choices redistribute demand. Crowded schools in one district can coexist with shrinking classes elsewhere. For an individual school, the local child population and whether families continue to move in may matter more than the national average.

4. Birth cohorts reach universities on a different timetable

People turning eighteen around 2036 were born around 2018. The 2018 statistical bulletin reported 15.23 million births; the 2008 bulletin reported 16.08 million. Comparing these historical releases gives a difference of about 5.3%, not a halving of the relevant population within ten years.

This is an age reference, not an admissions model. Statistical agencies revise population estimates, and births do not equal the number taking an entrance examination eighteen years later. Migration, survival, progression through school, repeat candidates, and admissions policy all matter. I would not present that 5.3% as a forecast for university intake in 2036.

I regard 2038–2045 as a period worth watching because smaller cohorts born in the 2020s will reach university age. That is a scenario based on cohort timing, not a validated forecast or a date on which every institution will begin competing for students. A future recovery in births cannot enlarge cohorts already born; it would primarily affect later intakes.

5. Today's universities still educate enormous numbers of students

The 2025 education bulletin reports 48.7257 million students across the various forms of higher education, including a reported postgraduate total of 4.2995 million. The Ministry reported 12.9 million registrations for the 2026 gaokao. Describing this system as already empty misrepresents its scale.

Total enrolment is not the size of a single entering class, and categories with different statistical definitions should not be added indiscriminately. Universities serve undergraduate, vocational, postgraduate, and continuing-education needs, which do not all move together.

Institutions must nevertheless reconsider their expectations. Campuses, departments, and staffing built during expansion face the prospect of smaller school-leaving cohorts. Better student–teacher ratios, continuing education, reorganised programmes, and contraction are different possible responses. Population constraints change the choices available; they do not make the policy decision.

6. Doctoral training is expanding; permanent faculty jobs need not follow

Doctoral admissions increased from 77,300 in 2016 to 201,600 in 2025, about 2.61 times the earlier figure. Master's admissions increased from 589,800 to 1,236,800 over the same period.

Admissions are not the number graduating that year, and doctoral students do not all seek faculty jobs. Expanded training can serve industrial research, medicine, and other professional work. Admissions growth alone does not establish an economy-wide surplus of PhDs. For someone interested only in a stable academic appointment, however, a doctoral degree cannot guarantee that vacancies will grow at the same rate.

Research, subject fit, project experience, and collaboration can all enter selection. Requirements differ across disciplines and institutions. Overseas experience, postdoctoral work, grants, and age conditions should not be assembled into a fictitious national checklist. Completing a doctorate and obtaining a long-term appointment remain distinct transitions.

7. Fewer new hires may matter before mass redundancies

Imagine a university that once replaced five retiring staff with five recruits. It might later recruit two, then suspend hiring in selected subjects. It could combine courses and roles or decline to renew some contracts. Over several years its workforce would change without a single dramatic announcement of mass dismissal. This is an illustrative scenario, not a claim that all universities have adopted it.

The effects would differ by career stage. Established faculty could face changed duties, while a doctoral graduate would encounter fewer vacancies before entering the institution. One position not advertised can alter that graduate's path without producing a visible rise in unemployment among established professors.

Budgets, retirement patterns, and actual recruitment notices would show where such adjustment is taking place. My judgment is that, if institutional expansion slows, the flow of new positions deserves attention before rumours about a collective clear-out.

8. Appointment review needs resources that match its rules

Fudan University's published 2025 spring work conference speeches discuss limited resources and positions alongside selection, career security, and support for long-term research. Selection and protection can coexist. The document does not establish that everyone will leave, or that appointment reform is a dismissal programme designed around population decline.

For junior faculty, the practical questions concern terms: whether standards are clear before entry, whether they can change midstream, whether assessment periods fit the discipline, whether promised funding and facilities arrive, and whether review disputes and transitions have reasonable procedures. Higher expectations without the means to meet them cast doubt on claims of fair competition.

Annual review, appointment-term assessment, and review for a long-term position are also different processes. I discussed that distinction in my earlier essay on faculty review and younger scholars. Individual circumstances still depend on the institution, role, and appointment documents.

9. Career transitions depend on skills and available jobs

Claims that academics will all end up in factories confuse manufacturing with assembly-line work and underestimate the difficulty of changing occupations. Some researchers in materials, electronics, computing, and automation can move into industrial R&D, engineering design, technical management, or commercialisation. Employers still need relevant skills and experience; a doctorate does not open those jobs automatically.

Researchers in the humanities and some basic sciences may pursue education, publishing, public institutions, consulting, or knowledge services. Those fields also impose qualifications and face limits on hiring. Listing them does not provide a guaranteed placement after an academic contract ends.

I do not want to disparage industrial work or pretend that transition costs are small. The useful questions concern the skills you already have, the ones you can acquire, and the jobs available where you can live.

10. Institutional risk will not follow a neat prestige ranking

A hierarchy in which elite universities survive, ordinary universities struggle, and all private institutions disappear is too tidy. Funding, location, subject mix, employment outcomes, and local demand all affect resilience. An applied university or vocational institution with strong industrial connections may attract students more successfully than an expensive programme with an unclear purpose.

Public universities also differ from institutions dependent on tuition income. Their authorities can choose mergers, departmental reorganisation, campus consolidation, or fiscal support rather than closure. Private institutions differ from one another too; ownership alone cannot settle the forecast.

I would look more closely where several vulnerabilities coincide: reliance on fees, continuing population outflow, unconvincing training, and weak graduate returns. Such institutions may face pressure earlier, but that requires institution-level investigation, not a countdown assigned to an entire category.

11. The next two decades will have overlapping stages

As a rough age-based guide, 2026–2032 invites attention to preschool and primary-school adjustment; 2033–2038 to smaller cohorts moving into secondary education and regional differences among universities; and 2038–2045 to cohorts born in the 2020s reaching higher-education age. Migration can bring pressure earlier or later for individual schools, so these intervals overlap.

Institutions competing for applicants may need to put more effort into financial aid, accommodation, changing majors, course quality, and employment support. More choice for students would be welcome. Smaller classes and better teaching still require resources, however; fewer students do not deliver those improvements by themselves.

Changes in aggregate demand also say little about access to a particular supervisor or sought-after programme. Fierce competition for a small number of opportunities can persist alongside institutions struggling to recruit.

12. The anxiety about another degree is also about work

An employment report carried on the Ministry's website puts the expected 2026 ordinary higher-education graduating cohort at 12.7 million. That is an estimate of graduates, not unemployed people. It establishes a large incoming cohort, not the employment result of any individual subject.

If desirable jobs do not expand alongside qualifications, some candidates may need an additional degree merely to retain their previous competitive position. That is one way to understand credential anxiety. Assessing a particular programme still requires graduate destinations, job content, income distributions, time commitments, and personal goals. National totals cannot determine individual returns.

Master's programmes can differ in training, research experience, and contact with employers. Education also has value beyond a first salary, including intellectual interests and the ability to make informed choices. Those benefits deserve recognition without being sold as guaranteed employment.

13. Less scarcity makes the detail behind a credential more important

As more candidates hold degrees, employers may distinguish between subjects, projects, internships, portfolios, and research contributions. Qualifications still convey information, but may do less of the selection work on their own. Further study can develop abilities or follow a rising recruitment threshold; the training itself helps distinguish those situations.

I do not regard wider access to higher education as a mistake. More people gaining the opportunity to study is a gain. Institutions and families nevertheless cannot promise returns based on the scarcity premium of a different era. An ordinary credential's supposed rarity is an increasingly weak assumption to carry into today's decisions.

14. University employment can mean very different working conditions

Long-term faculty, probationary faculty, postdoctoral researchers, project-funded employees, and teaching staff can already have different duties and protections within one university. Concentrated resources may widen those differences. One person receives sustained support while another searches for the next contract. Calling both situations stable academic employment hides too much.

Access to resources does not prove greater contribution, and a teaching role does not imply lower value. Foundational courses, shared facilities, and long-term scholarship may be poorly represented by short review cycles. Alongside observing occupational differences, we should ask whether institutions give different kinds of work appropriate recognition.

15. Prospective academics need to examine the flow of new positions

For someone planning to proceed from undergraduate study through a master's, doctorate, and postdoc into a faculty job, each transition deserves a fresh decision. Examine recent hiring in the target discipline, the contracts on offer, and the available resources before committing more years. A stable appointment after the doctorate should not be treated as a budgeted certainty.

This is not advice for everyone to leave research. Enthusiasm remains a reason to continue, but it must coexist with living conditions. Research, teaching, or industrial experience can broaden later choices. Institutions also owe applicants clear terms; they should not explain every institutional risk as a junior scholar's failure to work hard enough.

16. I expect divergence rather than a uniform collapse

Looking fifteen to twenty years ahead, I expect greater differences among universities, programmes, cities, and appointment types. Population, funding, industrial demand, and policy will interact. Some institutions may contract, others may change their role, and new educational needs can emerge.

Students should ask whether promised training materialises. Faculty should ask whether their work receives fair evaluation. Institutions should explain the basis for further expansion. A prediction of mass academic unemployment compresses those questions too far; an assurance that another degree always pays avoids them.

The title expresses my judgment, not a statistical finding that returns to every degree have fallen. Universities still conduct research, teach, and provide public services. The increasingly questionable promise is that opening another programme and awarding a higher credential will naturally attract sustained demand and deliver a safer career. Examining that promise helps with decisions we face now, without requiring a bet on universities disappearing.
