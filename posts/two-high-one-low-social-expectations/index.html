Two highs and one low: The gap between social expectations, grand narratives, and per-capita reality

Social expectations and grand narratives have both risen high, while the foundations of daily life have not improved at the same pace for many ordinary people. This essay examines that structural gap and the pressure it places on society.

In recent years, many people have asked the same question: where is Chinese society heading? The question appears in academic essays, graduates' job applications, household mortgage statements, civil service exam chat groups, and middle-aged adults' medical reports. Intense competition, enthusiasm for civil service jobs, cautious investment, and broad anxiety may look like separate phenomena, yet they share similar sources of pressure.

I think of that pressure as “two highs and one low.” The public and intellectuals alike have high expectations for better living conditions, secure social standing, and reliable institutions. Public messaging and grand narratives also speak at a high pitch. Yet per-capita income, social protection, access to education and health care, job security, and people's capacity to withstand shocks remain limited.

This shorthand helps explain a common experience. Many people acknowledge the country's aggregate achievements but struggle to turn them into a sense of personal security. People judge aggregate achievements through the concrete pressures of daily life.

The first high: Social expectations raised by decades of growth

Decades of rapid growth changed incomes and cityscapes. They also changed how people imagined the future. Homes would gain value, degrees would lead to better pay, cities would expand, and tomorrow would probably be better than today. Many families made decisions on the strength of that experience. Because it worked for years, it hardened into a shared assumption.

Economies do not follow a straight upward script forever. As growth slows, the wealth effect of property weakens, the wage premium for degrees declines, and competition for jobs intensifies, the old assumptions stop working. Expectations do not adjust at once. Living costs, mortgages, education spending, and status anxiety are already embedded in daily life.

Many people's “high expectations” now amount to a wish to hold on to what they have. Students fear graduating into unemployment. Employees at large technology companies worry about a career cliff after age 35. Middle-aged adults with families know that an illness, a layoff notice, or a fall in home prices could wipe out years of savings. Much of what is called involution is defensive competition driven by fear. “Lying flat” can be a withdrawn response to collapsed expectations.

Once history has raised expectations, calls to “be rational” cannot lower them. People know conditions have changed, but they do not know how to revise the plans on which they built their lives.

The second high: The volume of grand narratives

Public messaging about national rise, technological breakthroughs, infrastructure feats, and industrial upgrading has a factual basis. It tends to foreground aggregates, standout achievements, and the top of the pyramid, leaving the per-capita circumstances of ordinary people unexplained.

Aggregate achievements matter. Industrial capacity, infrastructure, and the state's organizational capacity make many basic improvements possible. If public messaging remains at the level of “we are strong” and “we won again,” while people face a difficult job market, slow income growth, heavy mortgages, and high education and health-care costs, a gap opens between the story and lived experience.

That gap also damages political language. People encounter triumphant public narratives alongside layoff notices, stagnant paychecks, and mounting pressure to manage family risks on their own. Without corresponding improvements in daily life, high-volume messaging becomes material for satire. Young people use sarcasm, dark humor, and memes to manage the resulting cognitive dissonance.

Public communicators can make a stronger case by acknowledging difficulties. They can explain how aggregate achievements coexist with household strain and connect future goals with present conditions.

The low: Per-capita reality hidden by aggregate figures

The “low” refers to per-capita reality. In aggregate terms, China has become a major industrial and infrastructure power. At the household level, many families still have little capacity to absorb risk.

Office towers in Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen, along with high-speed rail, mobile payments, and urban skylines, can create the impression of a fully developed country. China is too large for that image to hold. There are sharp differences among regions, between cities and the countryside, across industries, and among household balance sheets. Modern landscapes in a handful of places do not represent everyone's living conditions.

One serious illness can still overwhelm health coverage. Competition over education turns family resources into unequal opportunities for children, and job security remains weak. Many households hold most of their wealth in housing and have little spare cash flow. A public discussion a few years ago about the substantial number of people living on low monthly incomes struck a nerve because it interrupted a polished middle-class story: many households remain close to financial risk.

Infrastructure helps society function more efficiently, but it cannot replace the institutions that distribute income and provide social protection. A fast train cannot make mortgage payments for an unemployed worker. A beautiful skyline cannot pay a family's intensive-care bill. Concrete and steel offer only a partial image of modernity if they do not translate into stable expectations for ordinary people.

Why the pressure cooker starts to rattle

When expectations hang overhead, public messaging floats higher, and daily life grinds along at ground level, the social mood begins to resemble a pressure cooker. Officials may see an ungrateful public: after so much national development, why are there still so many complaints? Ordinary people may find grand narratives indifferent: if the country keeps winning, why am I still exhausted? Intellectuals caught between the two can feel trapped. They struggle to explain conditions to the public and to raise uncomfortable questions with those in power.

Different vantage points produce this mutual incomprehension. Aggregate achievements are substantial. The pressures recorded in household accounts and individual lives are concrete too. If each side speaks only from its own experience, each concludes that the other is unreasonable.

A way forward: Lower the volume, recalibrate expectations, and strengthen household security

More slogans will not solve the problem. Public messaging needs a more restrained tone, society needs to recalibrate expectations, and policy must address gaps in per-capita living conditions.

A more restrained tone means describing national achievements at a scale people can connect to their daily lives. Recalibrating expectations requires an honest account of the constraints linking the growth stage, job structure, industrial transition, and public welfare. The hardest task is directing more resources and policy attention toward social protection, health care and education, local public services, workers' rights, and families' capacity to withstand shocks.

Policies that improve per-capita living conditions would reduce the gap created by the two highs. High-volume narratives cannot sustain public morale for long, and people living in insecure conditions cannot remain optimistic on command. Stronger household security and more restrained public messaging would give people a firmer basis for setting expectations and relieve some of the pressure in society.

Recognizing the gap is the starting point for a serious discussion about the future.
