From expansion to adjustment: population, property, and policy feedback in a slower-growth era

A useful way to read many of China's social tensions over the past decade is to see a change of operating conditions. The economy that once absorbed policy errors through rapid expansion is now managing slower growth, older balance sheets, demographic contraction, and more contested expectations. Policy has become harder because every adjustment redistributes losses as well as opportunities.

This is an interpretive essay, not a single-cause explanation of a vast country. Population, housing, employment, local finance, and public administration each have their own institutions and regional differences. The claim is narrower: when the buffer of rapid expansion shrinks, delayed effects become more visible, policy objectives collide more often, and households respond to uncertainty in ways that can weaken the intended result.

When the growth buffer becomes thinner

During an incremental-growth era, new jobs, construction, land revenue, credit, and rising asset prices can move together. A growing pie does not solve unequal distribution or weak rules, but it can postpone the moment when those problems must be settled. Firms refinance, local governments fund new projects, households expect incomes and home values to rise, and young people can imagine that mobility will compensate for present pressure.

A period of adjustment works differently. Existing debts have to be serviced from slower-growing income. Housing is no longer assumed to appreciate everywhere. Public expenditure faces more claims from pensions, healthcare, childcare, debt service, and industrial policy. What once looked like a technical correction now decides which group carries the cost.

The latest numbers show the pressure without proving any one theory. According to the National Bureau of Statistics of China, China recorded 7.92 million births and 11.31 million deaths in 2025; people aged 60 or above accounted for 23.0% of the population. In the same year, GDP still grew by 5.0%. These facts belong in the same picture: continued economic capacity alongside a population structure that is changing faster than many institutions were designed to handle.

Population policy: from limiting births to supporting families

China's population policy has changed direction. Earlier restrictions aimed to slow population growth. Policy later moved through the two-child stage, and the 2021 three-child decision removed the social maintenance fee and called for supportive measures. In 2025, the country established a national childcare subsidy system for children under three. This is a substantive policy reversal, not merely a change in slogans.

The reversal also reveals the limits of treating fertility as an administrative target. A subsidy can lower a cost at the margin. It cannot by itself create stable employment, affordable housing, trusted childcare, equal career treatment for mothers, a tolerable education burden, or confidence that the support will remain in place for the next twenty years. Marriage and childbirth are long-horizon decisions made under uncertainty. Families price the whole life course, not only the payment available in the year of birth.

This helps explain why incentive language can sometimes produce resistance. People may hear a public objective where they expected a private choice. The problem is not that all support is manipulation. Paid leave, childcare, medical coverage, and cash assistance can expand real freedom. Support becomes less credible when it asks households to change behavior while leaving the largest risks with women and young families.

Property and finance: several objectives pulling on one system

Property once joined local land revenue, household wealth, developer financing, bank collateral, and urban construction into a powerful growth mechanism. The same connections accumulated leverage and made a housing slowdown relevant far beyond developers. Tightening leverage was therefore a response to genuine financial risk. It also acted on a system whose cash flow depended on continuing sales and confidence.

The popular story says that regulation pressed too hard and later policies had to “patch the hole.” That captures the visible reversal but compresses too much. Developer leverage, weak demand, presale obligations, demographic change, pandemic disruption, local dependence on land revenue, and expectations of falling prices interacted. No single measure explains the entire adjustment.

Policy consequently had to pursue goals that do not align perfectly: reduce financial risk, deliver presold homes, avoid indiscriminate credit support, stabilize expectations, and let unviable projects exit. The 2024 urban real-estate financing coordination mechanism illustrates this recalibration. It asked cities and financial institutions to identify projects eligible for financing while keeping lending market-based and ring-fenced.

Adjustment remains incomplete. The National Bureau of Statistics reported that real-estate development investment fell 17.2% in 2025 and sales area of newly built commercial property fell 8.7%. The question is no longer how to restore the old model unchanged. It is how to complete unfinished obligations, recognize losses, protect basic housing needs, and build local public finance that depends less on ever-rising land values.

Four adjacent pressures belong to the same transition

The first is a change in risk appetite. “Involution,” “lying flat,” interest in civil-service jobs, and more cautious consumption are not one unified movement. They do share a background: when returns to education, effort, and investment feel less predictable, people pay more for a secure floor and less for a distant promise.

The second is balance-sheet repair. Developers sell assets, local governments manage debt, businesses delay expansion, and households hold more liquid savings. Each action can be rational for the actor and contractionary in aggregate. The IMF's 2025 Article IV report connects the prolonged property adjustment and local-government debt overhang with weak domestic demand, while also noting the role of social protection in reducing precautionary saving. That is an institutional diagnosis, not a call for households to consume irresponsibly.

The third is aging before every part of the safety net is equally mature. Pension, healthcare, and long-term care obligations rise while the working-age share narrows. The pressure falls unevenly. Migrant workers, informal employees, only children supporting parents, and regions with different fiscal capacity do not encounter the same welfare state.

The fourth is friction between campaign-style administration and dispersed social knowledge. Target decomposition, deadlines, and concentrated resources can be effective for a bridge, vaccination drive, or emergency response. They are less reliable when the desired outcome depends on millions of private judgments about childbirth, hiring, investment, and consumption. A metric can compel a reported action without creating the confidence that makes the action durable.

A shared modern problem, with Chinese scale and institutions

Low fertility, expensive housing, aging, and weak youth confidence are not uniquely Chinese. The United Nations' World Population Prospects 2024 lists China, the Republic of Korea, Italy, and Spain among economies with fertility below 1.4. This comparison matters because it prevents every demographic outcome from being attributed to one political system.

Comparison also has limits. Countries differ in migration, gender relations, welfare provision, labor markets, housing finance, and local government incentives. The OECD's 2024 survey of Korea emphasizes career costs for mothers, youth finances, housing costs, and work-life balance. Cash benefits form only one part of that system. China's population size and centralized implementation can make a change faster and its spillovers wider. Shared symptoms do not imply identical causes or remedies.

What wuwei contributes, and what it cannot decide

Laozi's wuwei is often translated as nonaction. It does not mean total inactivity. The concept criticizes coercive, self-serving, and excessive action, and asks whether conduct follows the conditions from which order can emerge. A careful Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy account stresses both the contested translations and the connection between wuwei and ziran, or naturalness.

“Spontaneous order” is a useful modern bridge, but it is not Laozi's own term. Applied cautiously to public policy, the idea recommends stable boundaries, fewer arbitrary changes, room for local adaptation, and humility about what a central plan can know. It asks government to create conditions in which people can coordinate, rather than treating every household and firm as a component to be directly commanded.

Wuwei is not a complete policy manual. Pollution, monopoly, infectious disease, fraud, public goods, and unequal bargaining power do not disappear when government steps back. Rules, redistribution, and emergency capacity remain necessary. The practical lesson is restraint within action: intervene where collective problems require it, state the boundary clearly, and avoid converting a temporary instrument into permanent micromanagement.

Feedback is a better model than “one old pit, one new patch”

Social systems contain delays, nonlinear responses, and feedback loops. A housing restriction changes developer finance, household expectations, land revenue, bank behavior, and later construction. A fertility subsidy interacts with employment, childcare, housing, gender norms, and confidence in future policy. The result can arrive years after the initial decision.

Calling this “self-excited oscillation” can be a vivid control-theory metaphor, but it is not a settled scientific law of society. The safer claim is that delayed feedback makes overshoot and repeated correction possible. The OECD and IIASA's work on systemic policymaking similarly emphasizes feedback, uncertainty, experimentation, and adaptation when linear tools meet complex public problems.

Better governance in this setting is not passive. It defines the problem across departments, tests measures on a manageable scale, publishes assumptions, watches distributional effects, and changes course without rewriting the rules every few months. It gives temporary measures review dates and exit conditions. Most importantly, it asks who absorbs the adjustment cost before announcing an aggregate target.

The standard should be adaptive restraint

The past decade cannot be summarized as a simple failure of intervention or a morality tale about markets. China is moving from an era in which expansion concealed many contradictions into one in which existing claims must be reconciled. Population policy and property policy make that transition unusually visible because both reach deep into household expectations.

The quality of policy will depend less on the force of its initial launch than on its predictability, consistency, and capacity to absorb evidence. Families need support that reduces long-term risk. Housing adjustment needs loss recognition and credible completion of obligations. Young people need routes to security that do not require withdrawing from ambition. Public institutions need enough humility to distinguish a social response from administrative disobedience.

Read this way, wuwei is neither retreat nor romantic faith in an unregulated market. It is a discipline against unnecessary force. In a slower-growth society, governing well means knowing what must be done, what should be left room to evolve, and when yesterday's successful instrument has become today's source of friction.
