From expansion to adjustment: population, property, and policy feedback in a slower-growth era

A policy can solve an immediate problem and leave another cost to be dealt with later. Population and housing policy make this especially visible: decisions affect commitments that households carry for years. With slower income growth and existing debts to repay, changing direction becomes harder because someone has to absorb the loss.

Housing, employment, local finance, and fertility have different histories and vary across regions. I use them here to examine a shared difficulty: delayed effects are harder to manage when growth provides less room for adjustment. Households may respond cautiously to a new policy even when its designers want them to spend or invest.

When the growth buffer becomes thinner

Rapid growth can postpone difficult decisions. Firms refinance, local governments fund construction, and families expect future income to cover today's commitments. New jobs may make present inequality feel temporary. None of these expectations settles the underlying debt or distribution problem, but they can make it less urgent to those involved.

A period of adjustment works differently. Existing debts have to be serviced from slower-growing income. Housing is no longer assumed to appreciate everywhere. Public expenditure faces more claims from pensions, healthcare, childcare, debt service, and industrial policy. What once looked like a technical correction now decides which group carries the cost.

The latest numbers show the pressure without proving any one theory. According to the National Bureau of Statistics of China, China recorded 7.92 million births and 11.31 million deaths in 2025; people aged 60 or above accounted for 23.0% of the population. In the same year, GDP still grew by 5.0%. These facts belong in the same picture: continued economic capacity alongside a population structure that is changing faster than many institutions were designed to handle.

Population policy: from limiting births to supporting families

China's population policy has changed direction. Earlier restrictions aimed to slow population growth. Policy later moved through the two-child stage, and the 2021 three-child decision removed the social maintenance fee and called for supportive measures. In 2025, the country established a national childcare subsidy system for children under three. This is a substantive policy reversal, not merely a change in slogans.

A subsidy reduces some costs of raising a child. A family must still consider employment, housing, childcare, and the effect of care work on a parent's career. Support that lasts one year answers only part of a decision extending over many years. Its reliability matters alongside its amount.

An invitation to have children can sound intrusive if it treats a private decision chiefly as a means to meet a public target. Paid leave, childcare, health coverage, and cash assistance can still help people make the choices they want. Their value depends partly on whether they reduce the time and career risks borne by women and young families.

Property and finance: several objectives pulling on one system

Property once joined local land revenue, household wealth, developer financing, bank collateral, and urban construction into a powerful growth mechanism. The same connections accumulated leverage and made a housing slowdown relevant far beyond developers. Tightening leverage was therefore a response to genuine financial risk. It also acted on a system whose cash flow depended on continuing sales and confidence.

Describing the property adjustment as “regulation went too far, then policy patched the damage” identifies a reversal without explaining enough of it. Developer debt and presale obligations were already part of the problem. Weaker demand, demographic change, the pandemic, reliance on land revenue, and expectations of lower prices also affected cash flow.

Policy consequently had to pursue goals that do not align perfectly: reduce financial risk, deliver presold homes, avoid indiscriminate credit support, stabilize expectations, and let unviable projects exit. The 2024 urban real-estate financing coordination mechanism illustrates this recalibration. It asked cities and financial institutions to identify projects eligible for financing while keeping lending market-based and ring-fenced.

Adjustment remains incomplete. The National Bureau of Statistics reported that real-estate development investment fell 17.2% in 2025 and sales area of newly built commercial property fell 8.7%. The question is no longer how to restore the old model unchanged. It is how to complete unfinished obligations, recognize losses, protect basic housing needs, and build local public finance that depends less on ever-rising land values.

How households and institutions respond

Less predictable returns can make a secure income more attractive than a distant opportunity. That may help explain some interest in civil-service jobs or cautious consumption. “Involution” and “lying flat” describe varied responses, however, so I would not treat them as one coordinated movement.

Balance-sheet repair can reinforce that caution. Developers sell assets, local governments manage debt, businesses postpone expansion, and households keep liquid savings. These choices can make sense individually while reducing demand when made together. The IMF's 2025 Article IV report connects the prolonged property adjustment and local-government debt overhang with weak domestic demand. It also notes that social protection can reduce precautionary saving. Households' caution needs to be understood in those conditions.

Ageing adds demands for pensions, healthcare, and long-term care while the working-age share narrows. The available support differs. A migrant worker, an informal employee, and an only child caring for parents may face different gaps, as may residents of regions with unequal fiscal resources.

Breaking a task into targets and deadlines can help coordinate bridge construction or an emergency response. Decisions about having a child, hiring, or investing also depend on knowledge and expectations held by households and firms. Completing an administrative target does not establish that those people now have reason to make a lasting commitment.

A shared modern problem, with Chinese scale and institutions

Low fertility, expensive housing, aging, and weak youth confidence are not uniquely Chinese. The United Nations' World Population Prospects 2024 lists China, the Republic of Korea, Italy, and Spain among economies with fertility below 1.4. This comparison matters because it prevents every demographic outcome from being attributed to one political system.

Comparison also has limits. Countries differ in migration, gender relations, welfare provision, labor markets, housing finance, and local government incentives. The OECD's 2024 survey of Korea emphasizes career costs for mothers, youth finances, housing costs, and work-life balance. Cash benefits form only one part of that system. China's population size and centralized implementation can make a change faster and its spillovers wider. Shared symptoms do not imply identical causes or remedies.

What wuwei contributes, and what it cannot decide

Laozi's wuwei is often translated as nonaction. It does not mean total inactivity. The concept criticizes coercive, self-serving, and excessive action, and asks whether conduct follows the conditions from which order can emerge. A careful Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy account stresses both the contested translations and the connection between wuwei and ziran, or naturalness.

“Spontaneous order” is a modern comparison, not Laozi's own term. The comparison draws my attention to stable rules and room for local adjustment. A central plan cannot capture everything a household or business knows about its circumstances, so policy needs a way to learn from their responses.

Pollution, monopoly, infectious disease, and fraud still require public action; stepping back does not resolve unequal bargaining power or a shortage of public goods. I take wuwei here as a reason to question unnecessary intervention, not to dispense with rules or redistribution. A temporary measure should have a stated purpose and conditions for ending it.

Feedback is a better model than “one old pit, one new patch”

Social systems contain delays, nonlinear responses, and feedback loops. A housing restriction changes developer finance, household expectations, land revenue, bank behavior, and later construction. A fertility subsidy interacts with employment, childcare, housing, gender norms, and confidence in future policy. The result can arrive years after the initial decision.

Calling this “self-excited oscillation” can be a vivid control-theory metaphor, but it is not a settled scientific law of society. The safer claim is that delayed feedback makes overshoot and repeated correction possible. The OECD and IIASA's work on systemic policymaking similarly emphasizes feedback, uncertainty, experimentation, and adaptation when linear tools meet complex public problems.

A trial can specify the problem shared by several departments, publish its assumptions, and track who bears the costs. Its designers can then revise it against the results. Review dates and exit conditions help distinguish a temporary response from a rule that continues after its original purpose has passed.

Judge policy after the launch

Population and housing policy are difficult to change partly because families have already made commitments around them. Supporting a new choice and dealing fairly with an old obligation are separate tasks. A policy that addresses one may still leave the other unresolved.

For fertility support, ask whether it reduces a family's long-term uncertainty. For housing, ask whether outstanding obligations are completed and losses are recognized. Public institutions need evidence about these outcomes, including responses they did not intend, before deciding that people simply failed to follow the plan.

That is the restraint I find useful in wuwei: leave room to reconsider an intervention when the circumstances or its effects change. A policy's earlier success is a reason to study how it worked, not a reason to keep using it unchanged.
