Why leakage-controlled evaluation matters

This note explains one habit that appears across several of my current manuscripts: treat evaluation protocol design as part of the method, not as a final formatting detail.

The problem

A model can look better for reasons that have little to do with the proposed method. Normalization statistics may be computed on the full dataset. Retrieval memory may contain future samples. A time-series split may let events from the same storm, market period, or scene leak into both training and evaluation. These details can be small in code and large in interpretation.

Protocol-first habits

  • Use train-only statistics for normalization, calibration, and memory construction.
  • Prefer chronological or entity-level splits when samples are temporally or structurally coupled.
  • Document what information is available at prediction time, especially for retrieval and adaptation methods.
  • Report condition-dependent effects instead of forcing every result into a single improvement narrative.

Why it matters for current work

Leakage control is especially important in hurricane forecasting, trajectory prediction, financial forecasting, test-time adaptation, and retrieval-augmented models. These settings often have hidden structure: storms contain multiple horizons, trajectories share scene geometry, financial sequences are chronological, and adaptation methods can accidentally observe the wrong distributional signal.

Practical standard

My preferred public-facing standard is simple: state the split, state what is train-only, state what information the model sees at inference, and describe negative or conditional findings plainly. A careful protocol is not a decoration around a result. It is part of the result.
