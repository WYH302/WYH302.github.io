Why leakage-controlled evaluation matters

An apparent improvement can come from information the model should never have seen. In my current manuscripts, I treat the data split and the information available at prediction time as part of the method itself.

The problem

Computing normalization statistics on the full dataset gives a model access to the evaluation data. Retrieval memory can contain future samples. Even a time-series split may place observations from the same storm, market period, or scene in both training and evaluation. A few lines of preprocessing code can therefore change what a reported score means.

Protocol-first habits

I use training data to construct normalization statistics, calibration settings, and retrieval memory. If samples share a timeline or an entity, the split needs to respect that connection. For retrieval and adaptation methods, the protocol should state exactly what the model can access when it makes a prediction.

I also want to see results broken down by condition. An improvement that holds for one prediction horizon but disappears at another is worth reporting as such.

Why it matters for current work

These checks affect several tasks I study. Observations from one hurricane can appear at multiple forecast horizons, and trajectories can share the same scene geometry. Financial forecasting requires strict time ordering. Test-time adaptation and retrieval add further ways to expose a model to information that the stated task would not permit.

Practical standard

Before comparing scores, I check the split, the source of the normalization statistics, and the information available at prediction time. If the improvement disappears under another valid split, I would keep that result in the report. It helps explain where the method works and where the evidence is weaker.
