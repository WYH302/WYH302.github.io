Should AI rule, or should it audit power?

The appeal of an AI leader is easy to understand. People who have seen favoritism or rules rewritten for insiders want a decision-maker with no relatives to promote and no gifts to accept. I support using technology to constrain arbitrary power. An institution that makes decisions contestable offers more than a supposedly impartial ruler.

No gifts does not mean no bias

A model has no family ties in the human sense, but it uses data, labels, and objectives supplied by people. Historical promotion records that favor certain schools or networks can carry those preferences into a new score. Choosing which achievements count remains an exercise of judgment.

Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan's research on risk scores establishes that several formal fairness conditions cannot all hold outside constrained special cases. It does not prove that fairness itself is impossible. It gives us a reason to expose trade-offs rather than hide them inside a score.

An employer must decide how to weigh near-term output, long service, and potential under unequal starting conditions. An algorithm can help examine the consequences. The organization must still explain its choice.

Control over rules remains a form of power

Replacing a signatory with a model does not remove authority. Someone enters the data, decides what the system excludes, changes weights, and approves overrides. If the public sees only a final score, favoritism may become harder to detect.

People also adapt to incentives. Counting documents encourages more documents; counting visible hours encourages visibility. These are risks to address, not evidence that everyone will cheat. Assessments need checks against the work itself and room for contributions that the model cannot observe.

Use AI for oversight that others can verify

I would start with assistance in checking budgets, procurement, and appointment procedures: identify duplicate payments, compare changes in scores, and flag links for review. Every flag needs traceable supporting records. A relationship graph can justify a question; it cannot establish corruption or substitute for a disciplinary decision.

This work is not free. Missing data, mistaken identity matches, maintenance, and independent review require resources. Public records can contain personal information. Aggregation does not justify exposing unrelated private lives. Oversight should stay tied to public responsibilities, lawful purposes, and necessary access.

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework offers a reference for managing risks across design, use, and evaluation. The institutional measures below are my proposals, not a ready-made solution guaranteed by that framework.

The oversight system needs oversight too

Changes to criteria should record the reason, approver, and version, with an assessment of who is affected. The supplier, operating department, and appeals reviewer need separate roles. A challenged department should not be the sole judge of its own model.

People affected by an adverse decision need its main grounds, a way to correct data, and access to a reviewer empowered to change the result. Human review cannot be a confirmation button. Reviewers should give reasons, while institutions track false alarms, missed cases, and corrections, suspending unreliable uses where needed.

Open code can help scrutiny, but it does not reveal the deployed version, data quality, or exception approvals on its own. Restricted independent audits can address legitimate privacy or security concerns; secrecy also needs defined grounds and review. Employees, public representatives, and specialists should help debate rules. Majority voting alone cannot settle every question of rights.

Technology creates pressure, not inevitable checkmate

Cheaper tools give the public reasons to ask why previously difficult checks remain undone. Failure to deploy AI does not establish corruption: data readiness, legal limits, budgets, and error costs can be legitimate obstacles. Officials should explain those obstacles and propose bounded trials rather than invoke risk as a permanent veto.

Equally, installing an anti-corruption platform does not establish effective restraint. We need to know which levels it examines, whether flags lead to independent investigation, and whether outsiders can challenge its scope. The same technology can support public scrutiny or concentrated information control.

History offers conditions rather than guarantees

Britain's 1832 parliamentary reform illustrates the limits of redistribution of power. Parliament's historical account notes expanded middle-class representation without extending the franchise to the working classes. New economic interests, mobilization, and bargaining within existing institutions contributed to change; its benefits were uneven.

The arrival of a productive technology therefore does not guarantee greater power for ordinary people. AI can help them examine records, but access to records, a response to challenges, and correction of errors still need institutional protection.

I support reform through concrete trials: choose a bounded oversight task, publish the evaluation method, and compare errors and correction costs before and after adoption. Fairer governance requires procedures people can use, rather than another authority they cannot contest.
