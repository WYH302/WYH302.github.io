Language gravity: bias and expressive loss in AI compression

Developers encode criteria for what to preserve and discard through training data, loss functions, and human feedback. Once dominant forms occupy the high-probability center, systems are more likely to treat rare, colloquial, and institutionally undocumented experiences as noise.

The previous essay, “The Unsayable: Language as Lossy Compression of High-Dimensional Experience,” examined a basic constraint: experience loses some information when it enters a symbolic system. AI adds another layer of compression. Large language models learn which words appear more often and which sentences resemble a “normal answer,” then reshape new input to match those probabilities.

I call this tendency “language gravity.” Frequent vocabulary, standard grammar, and dominant value judgments form a probabilistic center. A model's rewrites, summaries, and translations drift toward it. The farther an expression sits from that center, the more likely the model is to remove its distinctive features and fill the gaps with familiar language.

Where language gravity comes from

Training corpora determine visibility first. Groups with the resources to publish, build databases, and maintain institutional websites leave more records in digital corpora. Dialect conversations, oral accounts, statements from temporary workers, and unarchived family memories rarely enter high-quality training sets. Models begin with a thinner record of these experiences.

Tokenization and probabilistic modeling widen the gap. Common words tend to map to stable token units, while rare names, dialect spellings, and mixed-language phrases may split into many pieces. Models learn language by predicting the next token, so frequent continuations have more influence on the loss function. Common decoding strategies favor high-probability paths, which makes rare expressions harder to preserve.

Human feedback changes the distribution again. If annotators prefer fluent, standard answers that match institutional language, the model learns a narrow definition of “good expression.” That standard can improve readability, but it can also classify hesitation, broken sentences, and unfamiliar narrative forms as low quality.

Compression reduces bandwidth and visibility

The phrase “low-dimensional expression by marginalized people” mistakes a property of the data for a property of the person. Their experiences are no less complex. When those experiences enter digital systems, they often leave shorter, fragmented, and sparsely annotated records. The training data reduces bandwidth, visibility, and context. It does not make the experience itself thinner.

Consider a worker describing unpaid wages through dialect, omission, and a broken chronology. AI can turn the account into a polished written complaint, but the rewrite may merge uncertain dates, remove terms tied to local labor relations, or turn speculation into fact. The text looks more like an institutional document, while the status of its evidence has changed.

Translation and summarization produce similar losses. A kinship term may encode age, responsibility, and social distance, while a standard translation retains only “family member.” A hesitant account may become a firm conclusion in a summary. Readers get smoother prose, but the speaker loses cues that expressed relationships and uncertainty.

Choices inside the distortion function

Rate-distortion theory uses d(x, x') to measure the difference between original information x and a reconstruction x'. Engineers try to minimize this distortion under a limited bit rate. Value judgments enter the same function in AI systems: platforms, annotators, and evaluation benchmarks determine which errors carry a high cost and which differences the system may ignore.

A summarization benchmark that checks only factual points and fluency will not penalize a model for deleting hesitation, identity markers, or the rhythm of a dialect. The model's score improves while it treats a group's speech patterns as “discardable error.” Average accuracy makes this loss hard to see.

When institutions use models to process complaints, medical narratives, social assistance applications, or content moderation cases, this distortion enters real decisions. Models are more likely to decode people who command standard written and institutional language as intended. Others must first reshape their experience to fit the system's format before they can enter the same process.

Reducing language gravity

A more reliable system should preserve the original text beside the model's rewrite and mark which content came from the source account and which came from the model's interpretation. For input that contains mixed languages, dialect, or gaps in the narrative, the system should preserve uncertainty, request more context, or offer multiple interpretations.

Evaluation should separate these cases. Alongside average accuracy, developers should report errors by language, dialect, expressive style, and resource scarcity. Community members should help define semantic loss. A benchmark may treat one word as a synonym, while a particular community uses it to mark generation, identity, or history.

AI can support communication across languages and help more people use the written formats institutions require. Those benefits depend on traceability and choice. Users should be able to see what the model changed and reject the rewrite.

When platforms select training data, annotation rules, and quality benchmarks, they also choose which information the system may discard. Developers need to disclose these choices and involve affected communities in defining distortion. Without that work, systems will keep rewriting diverse expressions into one central form. Users may gain access to a communication channel while losing the linguistic position from which they spoke.
