Language gravity: bias and expressive loss in AI compression

An AI rewrite can be easier to read while losing something the speaker meant. A hesitant date becomes definite; a local term becomes a generic one. I am interested in how a model decides which differences to preserve, especially when the original account does not resemble the formal writing common in its training data.

The previous essay, “The Unsayable: Language as Lossy Compression of High-Dimensional Experience,” examined a basic constraint: experience loses some information when it enters a symbolic system. AI adds another layer of compression. Large language models learn which words appear more often and which sentences resemble a “normal answer,” then reshape new input to match those probabilities.

I use “language gravity” for the tendency of rewrites, summaries, and translations to converge on familiar wording. It is a metaphor for a risk, not a measured force or a rule that every model follows. A fluent rewrite deserves a comparison with the original before we assume it preserved the meaning.

Where language gravity comes from

Groups able to publish, build databases, and maintain websites leave records that are easier to collect. A dialect conversation or an unarchived family account may leave no searchable text at all. If those accounts are missing from a training set, the model has less material from which to learn their wording and context.

Tokenization and probabilistic modeling widen the gap. Common words tend to map to stable token units, while rare names, dialect spellings, and mixed-language phrases may split into many pieces. Models learn language by predicting the next token, so frequent continuations have more influence on the loss function. Common decoding strategies favor high-probability paths, which makes rare expressions harder to preserve.

Human feedback can reinforce a preference for standard answers. If annotators consistently favour polished institutional prose, a model may remove hesitation or unfamiliar narrative forms to match that preference. Readability can improve even while a meaningful distinction disappears.

Compression reduces bandwidth and visibility

Calling someone's expression “low-dimensional” confuses the record with the person. A brief or fragmented account may reflect limited time, access, or annotation. It does not show that the experience behind it was simple.

Consider a worker describing unpaid wages through dialect, omission, and a broken chronology. AI can turn the account into a polished written complaint, but the rewrite may merge uncertain dates, remove terms tied to local labor relations, or turn speculation into fact. The text looks more like an institutional document, while the status of its evidence has changed.

Translation and summarization produce similar losses. A kinship term may encode age, responsibility, and social distance, while a standard translation retains only “family member.” A hesitant account may become a firm conclusion in a summary. Readers get smoother prose, but the speaker loses cues that expressed relationships and uncertainty.

Choices inside the distortion function

Rate-distortion theory uses d(x, x') to measure the difference between information x and its reconstruction x' under a limited bit rate. I use this as an analogy for evaluation choices in AI. A platform or benchmark designer decides which errors count heavily; differences left out of that evaluation may receive little protection.

If a benchmark measures only factual points and fluency, it may miss the loss of hesitation, identity markers, or dialect. A summary could therefore score well while changing how a speaker presents their knowledge or relationships. An average score would not tell us which distinctions were lost.

Those changes matter if the rewritten text enters a complaint review, a medical record, or a social assistance decision. A person whose account already fits the expected format may need fewer corrections. Others may have to revise the model's version before an institution has even considered what they wanted to say.

Reducing language gravity

A more reliable system should preserve the original text beside the model's rewrite and mark which content came from the source account and which came from the model's interpretation. For input that contains mixed languages, dialect, or gaps in the narrative, the system should preserve uncertainty, request more context, or offer multiple interpretations.

Evaluation should separate these cases. Alongside average accuracy, developers should report errors by language, dialect, expressive style, and resource scarcity. Community members should help define semantic loss. A benchmark may treat one word as a synonym, while a particular community uses it to mark generation, identity, or history.

I still see value in helping someone translate an account or prepare it in a required written format. The user should be able to compare versions and reject a change without losing access to the service.

Developers should explain what their quality tests measure and invite affected speakers to identify omissions those tests miss. I would want to know whether a rewritten complaint kept an uncertain date uncertain, or whether a translated term still conveyed the relationship the speaker intended. Those checks are more informative than calling the final prose fluent.
