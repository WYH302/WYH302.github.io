I do not believe the future of robotics has to be humanoid

I keep coming back to one question about humanoid robots: why must they look like us? Before discussing how steadily a robot walks or how fast it runs, I want to understand why we chose that body.

I understand the appeal. A machine stands up, turns toward you, and reaches out a hand; it is easy to feel that the future has arrived. But that feeling cannot replace an engineering reason. Building a useful machine and realizing a familiar image sometimes lead to the same design. I worry that we too often assume they must.

Learning from humans does not require copying the whole body

Our ability to create technology does not show that every part of the human body is the best structure for a task. Admiring human cognition does not establish a need for a human torso, legs, head, and face. We have the bodies we inherited. We can choose the bodies we build for robots, so I would start with the work.

Consider a robot working on a level indoor floor, moving between workstations to pick up objects, assemble parts, or put things away. My starting point would be a wheeled base and a manipulator. Under those conditions, the need for two legs is not obvious. What problem would legs solve that wheels cannot, and how much added complexity in control, maintenance, and safety would that capability justify?

Wheels are not suitable everywhere. I am not asking anyone to prove that one design wins in every setting. I want a specific explanation of what a human-shaped body contributes to the intended job. “That is how people walk” is not enough. Neither is “this is the future.”

I care more about understanding and manipulation

I am in no hurry to meet a mechanical version of a human. I would rather have a machine that understands what I want done, assesses the situation in front of it, and completes the work reliably.

Picking something up is different from choosing where to grasp it. Moving an object is not the end of the job if the robot cannot check whether it is resting securely. If something unexpected happens, does it keep executing the old motion or stop to reassess? Can it notice a slipping object? Can it recover safely from a failed attempt without waiting for someone to clean up?

That is why I would put more research effort into the robot's “brain” and its ability to manipulate objects. By brain, I mean perception, understanding, planning, learning, and error detection, without requiring a biological copy of the human brain. Manipulation includes making contact, controlling force, coordinating actions, and checking that the task is complete.

For many of the jobs I care about, handling objects creates the value. I do not need an unverified “99%” claim to make that point. Nor can the rest of the body be an afterthought: a base must support an extended loaded arm, the mechanism must reach the required heights, and sensors must observe the operation. I want to remove unnecessary demands to imitate humans, not these engineering requirements.

My preferred robot might look nothing like a person

It might have a wheeled base with a low centre of gravity, a height-adjustable column, and one or two arms. The work would determine the number. The body could be round or take any shape that makes loading, movement, and maintenance practical. I see no reason to add a chest, shoulders, and waist just to complete a silhouette.

It need not have five fingers. If a gripper performs the task reliably, use a gripper. If mounting a tool directly makes more sense, mount the tool. I would pay for the complexity of a multi-fingered hand when it provides a capability the job needs.

The same applies to a head. Put sensors where they can see the workspace; use a rotating mount if they need to look around. If that arrangement happens to resemble a head, fine. There is no need to decide that a robot must have one and then invent its purpose. I do not need it to have a face.

Such a machine might be less spectacular, more equipment than science-fiction character. But why should equipment resemble a character? If it can sort objects, assemble parts, and handle repetitive work reliably, the absence of legs would not make it less advanced to me. Solving a problem with a simpler mechanism deserves respect.

A demonstration supports only what it shows

An impressive motion makes it easy to imagine abilities far beyond the motion itself. Walking does not establish fitness for a job. Picking up an object once does not show that the robot will succeed after the object or its position changes. Whether a complex action demonstrates task understanding depends on the information and conditions that made it possible.

After watching a video, I want to know whether the robot acted autonomously or through teleoperation. How many attempts were made, what did failures look like, and how much help did people provide? During sustained operation, how much time goes to charging, maintenance, and resets? Could simpler equipment do the same work, and if so, why choose this design?

I would not call a project deceptive just because it uses a humanoid body. Wheeled robots deserve the same scrutiny. My concern is presenting an experiment as a product, an occasional success as dependable performance, or assisted operation as full autonomy.

Fundraising, valuations, and listings are separate questions. I have no objection to a business solving a real problem and earning repeat customers. But raising money for a story does not show that a machine can do the work. Commercial attention cannot substitute for technical evidence.

Humanoid form should be a conclusion reached through comparison

Legged or other locomotion deserves study when the terrain defeats a wheeled base. I would also accept a human-like structure if a particular application shows that it reduces environmental modifications, covers the required tasks, and offers better reliability and overall cost.

“The environment was built for humans” is a serious consideration, but it does not establish the need to copy the entire body. Reaching a human working height need not require human legs. Using a tool need not require a complete copy of a hand. Looking around need not require a face. Each component should earn its place through its contribution to the task.

I am sceptical of treating the complete human form as the default for a general-purpose robot. Looking more like us does not by itself mean becoming more advanced. I would develop dependable understanding and manipulation, then select a body that supports them. Some humanoid robots may prove valuable; that is a narrower claim than saying the future of robotics is humanoid.

Robots cannot arrive through a “Great Leap Forward” either

I have a similar doubt about an overly smooth timetable: a new model appears yesterday, someone puts it in a mechanical body today, and tomorrow it understands the world, handles many kinds of work, and enters every home. The missing work may be the hardest part.

A model demonstrating an ability and a complete machine reliably doing a job are different achievements. I want to know how far apart they are: which problems have been solved, which approaches look promising, and which abilities still lack an adequate test. A story that jumps from the beginning to the destination leaves those questions unanswered.

Respecting researchers does not make them prophets

I am willing to listen carefully to Fei-Fei Li or Geoffrey Hinton. Respecting their research contributions does not require accepting every prediction about the future. Titles such as “godfather” or “godmother” of AI do not supply evidence for a specific technical judgment.

That does not make a non-specialist's intuition superior to expert knowledge. The less I understand a subject, the more I need to know the evidence and untested assumptions behind a prediction. I can support a research direction while doubting a timetable, or recognize a result's importance while asking what remains unresolved.

I resist treating questions as a lack of faith. This is my approach to technical predictions, not an allegation about a particular statement by either researcher. A vision should remain open to examination without requiring agreement in advance.

My objection is to using determination in place of conditions

I use “Great Leap Forward” to criticize an approach that rushes toward a desired outcome. I am not equating contemporary research with that political campaign or its disastrous consequences. In its 1981 resolution on Party history, the Chinese Communist Party discussed overconfidence in subjective effort, inadequate understanding of actual conditions, and the absence of careful investigation and pilot work.

The lesson I take is that strong wishes cannot replace the conditions needed to achieve a goal. Mobilizing more people and resources still leaves the technical questions to answer. An urgency to overtake others does not justify announcing success before demonstrating it.

I support sharing research and dividing work among teams. I also accept that research can require substantial resources and risk failure, provided its sponsors describe it as exploration. Cooperation is not obedience, and concentrated investment is no guarantee of success.

I object to explanations that cannot be tested: if success has not arrived, the answer is always too little money, compute, or participation, while the underlying hypothesis remains beyond question. Those resources can be constraints. But we should also allow the possibility that we have not found the right method. Researchers should explain what outcome would lead them to revise their judgment.

Compute matters, but it does not answer every question

More data and larger training runs can help. I question the inference that spending more on them will resolve the other difficulties of general-purpose robotics. Looking at a specific study helps reveal the work between an idea and a result.

In its 2023 introduction to RT-2, Google DeepMind described combining web and robotics data. The RT-1 demonstration data used in that work came from 13 robots over 17 months in an office kitchen. The researchers then evaluated RT-2 through more than 6,000 robotic trials. Data collection and evaluation are distinct stages, and these numbers describe different parts of the work.

This does not mean that every later project must take as long, or that these experiments established readiness for general deployment. It shows how much accumulated work can sit behind a result that appears sudden in a short video.

World Labs also discusses the cost of real-device data collection in its account of robot training and simulation. People must reset objects, recover from failures, and maintain equipment. Training needs to cover physical properties, robot states, and failure conditions. Simulation can expand training, but researchers still need to check the gap between simulated environments and real operations.

I therefore want to know which gap the added data fills, which ability the added compute improves, and whether the improvement holds in a new real-world setting. If a robot lifts a cup of water, I want more than the successful attempt. What happens if the cup moves, contains more water, slips during grasping, or a person reaches into the workspace?

A robot need not handle every situation. It should specify which situations it promises to handle and how it stops or asks for help outside that range. A clear operating boundary is more useful to a user than a claim of “powerful capabilities.”

A breakthrough can accelerate progress, but it cannot replace validation

Developing robots step by step does not mean making every research project wait in a queue. Teams can work in parallel, change methods, and discover approaches that solve several problems at once. Progress may come faster than expected. An ability that has not been tested still cannot be treated as reliable.

For a delivery promise, I want evidence that the machine can complete the task repeatedly, cope with the environmental variation it claims to support, and detect and handle errors safely. Sustained use also has costs and requirements for human support. These are questions I use to judge a promise, not a prescribed sequence for every research programme.

I do not expect basic research to earn money at once. A paper can address one small problem, and a prototype can remain far from everyday use. I can accept either if the team explains its status. I cannot accept claiming to have solved a whole task after a partial experiment, or omitting restricted conditions and continuing human assistance from the account.

I do not know how long general-purpose robotics will take. Doubting an optimistic forecast does not entitle me to invent an unsupported pessimistic one. I will welcome progress and change my mind when results warrant it. Sustained work, safe recovery, disclosed costs, and a fair comparison with alternatives would persuade me more than a grand promise.

I want a machine that gets better at doing the work. Get the task right first, then consider whether the body needs to look human. And leave the unresolved problems in the story of how we get there.
