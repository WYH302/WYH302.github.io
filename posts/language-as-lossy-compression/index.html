The unsayable: language as lossy compression of high-dimensional experience

What we can say is often a projection left after symbolic encoding compresses experience. Ancient thinkers encountered this boundary through contemplation and practice. Information theory and representation learning now let us describe some of its mechanisms.

Laozi writes, “The Dao that can be spoken is not the constant Dao; the name that can be named is not the constant name.” The old parable of the blind men and the elephant offers another version: each person touches a real part but mistakes it for the whole. Zen's caution against relying on written words addresses the same problem. Words can point toward experience, but they are not the experience itself.

These ideas need no mystical framing. They begin with a sober judgment about language: naming makes the world available for discussion, and the act of naming removes detail at the same time.

Experience is wider than a sentence

When you see a tree, your field of view contains light, color, edges, distance, and bodily posture, along with memory and emotion. You say, “I see a tree.” The listener receives a useful summary but cannot reconstruct the full experience from that sentence.

“High-dimensional” does not need to mean a set of mathematical dimensions that we can count one by one. It means that experience contains many degrees of freedom that change at once. Language unfolds in a linear sequence. Grammar and context can express complex relationships, but sentence length, vocabulary, and shared background remain limited. Language must select what to preserve.

An irreversible encoding

Let x represent present experience and E represent linguistic encoding. The spoken sentence is y = E(x). The listener uses another process, D, along with memory and context to reconstruct an experience x' = D(y). In most cases, x' will not match x in full.

A stronger constraint appears when different experiences compress into the same sentence. Two people may both say, “I feel sad,” while their bodily sensations, memories, and reasons differ. In mathematical terms, E(x1) = E(x2) does not imply x1 = x2. The encoding has merged those differences, and the listener cannot recover them from the sentence alone.

Information theory studies this tradeoff through rate-distortion theory. Under a limit on code length, an encoder preserves information relevant to the task and accepts some reconstruction error. A subway map removes street widths, building appearances, and true distances while preserving transfer relationships. That distortion helps a passenger navigate.

High-dimensional vectors remain task-shaped representations

Modern machine learning maps words, images, and sounds into high-dimensional vectors. These representations can preserve similarity, category, and contextual relationships, which supports search, classification, and generation. No matter how many dimensions a vector has, training objectives, data, and model architecture still shape what it represents.

A model preserves distinctions that reduce its loss function and ignores details outside the training task. High-dimensional vectors still omit information, and a computable representation may capture correlation without anything more. Each vector space reflects the scales its designers chose.

What mathematics explains

Computer science has not “proved” that Laozi or Zen thinkers already understood information theory. Philosophical claims and mathematical theorems use different systems of argument. Modern theory offers computable models for several old intuitions: expression requires selection, compression creates distortion, and a receiver cannot reconstruct one unique original experience from finite symbols.

The same constraint helps explain why language works. Communication would fail if each sentence had to copy an entire world. Concepts remove differences and let us categorize, reason, and collaborate. We can judge a linguistic compression by asking whether the missing information affects the task at hand.

Acknowledge the boundary and keep reasoning

Acknowledging the limits of language makes an argument more honest. Speakers need to know what their account leaves out, and listeners should not treat one sentence as the whole fact. Complex experiences call for more perspectives and longer context. Some also require direct practice.

Modern mathematics does not certify ancient thought. It lets us examine the same class of limits through another symbolic system. Ancient thinkers encountered language's boundary through experience, practice, and debate. Computer scientists measure it through encoding, compression, and representation. Their paths cross here and give us reason to handle our models, concepts, and conclusions with greater care.
