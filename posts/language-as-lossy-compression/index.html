The unsayable: language as lossy compression of high-dimensional experience

“I see a tree” tells a listener something, but leaves out almost everything about the light, the distance, and how it felt to be there. I find lossy compression a useful analogy for that gap. It helps describe what a sentence preserves without pretending to measure the whole experience.

Laozi writes, “The Dao that can be spoken is not the constant Dao; the name that can be named is not the constant name.” I read that alongside the parable of the blind men and the elephant, and Zen's caution about relying on written words. The traditions have different concerns, but they prompt a question I share: how much can a description tell us about something we have not experienced?

Naming lets us discuss things without reproducing every detail of an encounter. The listener supplies much of what is missing from memory and context. That is useful, though it also makes misunderstanding possible.

Experience is wider than a sentence

When you see a tree, your field of view contains light, color, edges, distance, and bodily posture, along with memory and emotion. You say, “I see a tree.” The listener receives a useful summary but cannot reconstruct the full experience from that sentence.

I use “high-dimensional” here for the many aspects of an experience that vary together, not for a measured number of dimensions. A sentence presents information in sequence. Grammar and shared context let us express complex relationships, but we still choose which details deserve the available words.

An irreversible encoding

Let x represent present experience and E represent linguistic encoding. The spoken sentence is y = E(x). The listener uses another process, D, along with memory and context to reconstruct an experience x' = D(y). In most cases, x' will not match x in full.

A stronger constraint appears when different experiences compress into the same sentence. Two people may both say, “I feel sad,” while their bodily sensations, memories, and reasons differ. In mathematical terms, E(x1) = E(x2) does not imply x1 = x2. The encoding has merged those differences, and the listener cannot recover them from the sentence alone.

Information theory studies this tradeoff through rate-distortion theory. Under a limit on code length, an encoder preserves information relevant to the task and accepts some reconstruction error. A subway map removes street widths, building appearances, and true distances while preserving transfer relationships. That distortion helps a passenger navigate.

High-dimensional vectors remain task-shaped representations

Modern machine learning maps words, images, and sounds into high-dimensional vectors. These representations can preserve similarity, category, and contextual relationships, which supports search, classification, and generation. No matter how many dimensions a vector has, training objectives, data, and model architecture still shape what it represents.

Training encourages a model to preserve distinctions that help its task. Other details may receive little attention. More vector dimensions do not guarantee that the representation captures everything relevant to a person, or that a learned correlation amounts to understanding.

What mathematics explains

The comparison with information theory is an analogy, not a proof of an ancient philosophical claim. A mathematical model can specify an encoding and measure distortion under stated assumptions. It cannot, on that basis alone, establish what Laozi or Zen thinkers meant by the limits of language.

Leaving things out is also how language remains usable. A passenger needs a station name and a route, not a complete description of the city. In a different conversation, the omitted detail may matter. I would judge the description by whether it preserves what the listener needs for the task.

Acknowledge the boundary and keep reasoning

A speaker can help by making room for questions and explaining a detail that the first account left out. A listener can avoid treating one sentence as the whole event. Longer context may resolve the gap; learning a practical skill may also require doing it rather than reading another explanation.

The compression analogy gives me a way to ask what a description has left out and whether the omission matters. I would keep it at that scale. It is useful for examining a sentence or a model without treating either as a complete account of experience.
